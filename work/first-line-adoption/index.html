Segmenting 3,500 prescribers for a first-line launch

Two simple measures sorted 3,500 prescribers into four groups. In the field program built on them, 33% of targeted prescribers with a first-line opportunity started a patient, compared with a 24% historical average.

My roleData Analyst II · Bristol Myers Squibb
Methods & toolsCommercial data, Python, SQL, reporting and field measurement

The problem

A hematology brand known as a second-line treatment was approved for first-line use. The team needed to know which prescribers were already using it earlier in treatment and where habits were holding adoption back. I owned the analytics: segmentation, targeting measurement and a weekly competitive readout.

What I built

I narrowed roughly 9,400 prescribers to about 3,500 who started first-line patients in the launch window. Then I split them on two measures the brand team already used: the brand’s share of first-line new patients and its use across treatment lines.

That gave four groups: first-line majority, first-line occasional, second-line users and non-users. Second-line users and non-users held 1,224 of the 1,547 patient opportunities, about 79%.

I chose explicit rules over clustering because field teams had to explain a segment quickly and tie each one to a different conversation.

What happened

Marketing built messaging for each group, and the field team targeted about 1,000 prescribers, mostly second-line users and non-users, with four calls over three months.

Non-users getting more than one call a month went from 18% to 45%. Of 194 targeted prescribers with a first-line opportunity, 64 started a patient: 33%, compared with a 24% average over the prior four quarters.

What I would do differently

This is a historical comparison, not a controlled test, so it does not prove the program caused the difference. With only about a year of data, holding out a region or validating against a control group was not practical. As more data built-up, my next step would have been a driver model that separates the program's effect from market shifts and targeting, to pinpoint what actually caused the difference.
