Explaining monthly sales with regression

A monthly regression framework for an established specialty-care brand.

The useful model was the one that explained a monthly business question, even when a rolling three-month version fit better.

My roleDecision Analytics Associate · ZS Associates
Methods & toolsRegression, Python, commercial data, diagnostic reporting

Anonymized professional case study. Client and product identities are omitted. Charts are replotted from the original model records with generic labels. Sales volumes and response-curve axes are indexed; raw datasets and original source files are not published.

The question

For an established specialty-care brand, the recurring question was why sales had risen or fallen compared with the previous month or the same month a year earlier. The team also wanted to inspect how specific inputs related to performance.

I worked on the driver model and its interpretation for commercial stakeholders. This was diagnostic modeling, with a recurring refresh process.

Build predictors around plausible mechanisms

The monthly regression included selling days, payer mix, lagged demand, ordering-account counts, earlier treatment use, field activity, price-related stock-up effects, seasonality and treatment timing.

Calls used a logarithmic relationship; the other modeled inputs used linear forms. A stock-up feature helped represent behavior around an announced price change rather than treating every sales spike as underlying demand growth.

The key choice was the time window

We compared monthly modeling with a rolling three-month view. Smoothing improved fit, but blurred the month-specific changes the diagnostic needed to explain.

We chose monthly granularity. That made the model less flattering as a fit statistic, but more useful for the question being asked. I would describe this as a tradeoff in diagnostic resolution, rather than an improvement in predictive accuracy.

Give the model a clear operating cadence

Keeping coefficients fixed between refreshes made month-to-month diagnostic comparisons easier to interpret.

Monthly diagnostics decomposed changes using fixed coefficients. Quarterly updates re-estimated the model with the additional quarter. Annual reviews reconsidered the variables and reference window, with out-of-cycle refreshes when required.

Three ways the output was used

The waterfall above shows one diagnostic comparison from the original deck. The prior period is indexed to 100, and the model-based driver contributions reconcile to the next-period total. The largest positive contribution is the modeled price-related stock-up effect.

  1. Diagnostic waterfalls: break a period-to-period change into modeled driver contributions.
  2. Tactic scenarios: vary an input while holding the other modeled conditions fixed.
  3. Response curves: sweep a driver through its observed range around a reference month to inspect the fitted relationship.

These outputs gave stakeholders a consistent language for discussing performance. They were not proof that changing a tactic would cause the modeled sales response.

Inspect the response before recommending a tactic

The call curve has diminishing returns in the fitted model. Earlier treatment use has a linear relationship over the plotted range. These scenarios show what the model predicts under fixed inputs; they do not establish the causal effect of changing a tactic.

Limits I would state explicitly

This was correlational attribution. Controls for time and observed covariates do not remove every confounder. Demand can influence field activity as well as the reverse, and correlated predictors can make individual contributions unstable.

For a high-stakes investment decision, I would pair this diagnostic with experimental evidence or a defensible quasi-experimental design. I would also examine coefficient stability, residual autocorrelation and out-of-time performance before using the model for forward forecasts.
